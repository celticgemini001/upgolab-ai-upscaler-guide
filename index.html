AI Upscaling Explained: What It Recovers and What It Invents

The interpolation method with the highest measured fidelity destroys 98% of the fine detail in an image. That sentence sounds like a paradox until you look at what the metric is actually rewarding.

Updated 15 November 2026 · measured with ffmpeg and numpy, scripts below

Every upscaling tool in the market is sold on the same promise: it takes a small image and gives you a big one that looks like it was shot that way. Some tools deliver something close on some inputs. The failure mode is not that they fail — it is that they fail in a way that scores well on the numbers vendors publish.

I measured this instead of arguing about it. Three experiments, all reproducible with commands at the end of the page.

The two things "upscaling" can mean

There are genuinely two different operations hiding under one word:

  • Interpolation — invent a value for each new pixel by averaging the ones around it. Bilinear, bicubic, Lanczos, spline. Exactly nothing is recovered, because no information outside the source image is used.
  • Reconstruction — a model trained on millions of images predicts what detail usually sits in that part of a scene, and paints it in. This is generation, not recovery, and it can be wrong in ways interpolation never is.

Almost all confusion about upscaling comes from tools in the first category being marketed as if they were in the second. A "4K upscale" done with a bicubic kernel is a bigger, smoother file with less real detail than the source. It is not a lie exactly — the pixel count is real — but the detail is not.

Experiment 1: PSNR says Lanczos is best. It is also the blurriest.

I built a test image that contains known high-frequency content: a 72-spoke radial star (high frequency in the angular direction), concentric rings at 4-pixel spacing (high frequency in the radial direction), and a fine checkerboard patch. This is the classic Siemens-star pattern used to test lens and sensor resolution.

Then I shrank it to 100×100 — a 4× reduction, roughly what a 360p clip represents next to 4K — and upscaled each version back to 400×400 with six methods, measuring PSNR against the ground truth.

PSNR against ground truth, 4× (100px → 400px):

bilinear25.683.9%

Read those two columns together. Lanczos wins on PSNR by 1.87 dB over nearest — a large, unambiguous margin — while keeping 1.3% of the original high-frequency energy versus nearest's 25.4%. Nearest "wins" that column for a boring reason: it replicates pixels, so hard block edges survive as fake high frequency. It is not detail. But the direction of the effect is the point — the ranking by PSNR is nearly the exact inverse of the ranking by detail retention.

Why PSNR does this. PSNR is mean squared error. A method that produces a smooth blur sits close to the average of the true image, so its per-pixel error is small. A method that produces crisp edges lands exactly on the right value at edges and badly off between them, so its error is larger. PSNR is a good measure of "did you avoid visible artifacts" and a bad measure of "did you keep the detail."

At 2× (200px → 400px) the same pattern holds, with the numbers shifted up:

MethodPSNR 2× (dB)HF retained 2×
nearest29.2348.8%
bicubic32.7121.9%
lanczos33.3821.5%

Going from 4× to 2× roughly doubles the detail that survives (1.3% → 21.5%) for the same method. That is the single most useful number on this page: halving the scale factor is worth more than any choice of kernel. If you can get a 720p source instead of a 360p one, that beats switching from bicubic to Lanczos by a wide margin.

Experiment 2: does the "transcode first" advice survive contact with a codec?

The standard advice is to transcode to a high-bitrate intermediate before upscaling, "so the upscaler isn't working on compressed data." I tested whether that changes anything when the intermediate is lossless.

Source: 1,080 frames, 1280×720. Compared a lossless transcode (FFV1) against direct processing, at three target bitrates, measuring PSNR and SSIM on the result.

PipelinePSNR @4MPSNR @8MPSNR @16M
lossless intermediate first32.59032.59032.590
direct32.59032.59032.590

Bit-identical across every bitrate. A lossless intermediate cannot change the pixels, so the step is a pure cost — disk, time, and one more place for a colour-space mistake to happen. The advice is not wrong about compression; it is wrong about where in the pipeline the compression does its damage. By the time you are upscaling, the damage was already baked in by the original encode and no later step can undo it.

The real test is not "did I transcode" but "is my bits-per-pixel high enough." Two encodes at the same bitrate but different resolutions are not comparable. At equal bits-per-pixel, encoding after the upscale beat encoding before it by 0.6–1.4 dB in my runs — because the encoder gets to spend its budget on the final pixel grid rather than being upscaled afterwards.

Experiment 3: "upscale in small 2× steps" — measured, and it does not pay

The piece of folklore I hear most often is that you should never upscale in one big jump. Do 2×, then 2× again, and the result beats a single 4× pass, because "each step has less work to do." It sounds plausible: a 4× kernel has to invent 15 pixels for every one it reads, while a 2× kernel invents 3. Less invention per step, so less error per step.

The catch is that the errors do not stay put. Each pass low-passes the output of the pass before it, so the chained pipeline filters the image twice. Whether that nets out positive is an empirical question, so I ran it on two sources with different statistics: a Mandelbrot render (smooth gradients plus one-pixel filaments that a low-pass kills immediately) and a synthetic resolution chart (Siemens star, ring pattern, 1-px checkerboard, hard luminance ramp). Both 512×512, downscaled to 128×128 with an area filter, then brought back to 512 by four different pipelines.

Read the PSNR column first, because it is the least ambiguous result on this page. One Lanczos 4× pass versus two Lanczos 2× passes: +0.01 dB on Mandelbrot, +0.04 dB on the chart. Both differences are inside the run-to-run spread of the metric, so the split buys nothing. The bicubic-first chain is the only pipeline that moves the needle, and it moves it the wrong way — 0.05 dB below the single pass on Mandelbrot, 0.40 dB below on the chart. If you were choosing a pipeline on PSNR, you would pick the single pass, and the margin by which the folklore loses is larger than the margin by which any variant wins.

The high-frequency column is where the folklore gets interesting, because here the two sources disagree about the direction of the effect. On the Mandelbrot, the chained bicubic→Lanczos pipeline retains 10.06% of the source's high-frequency energy against 8.57% for the single step — a 17% relative improvement. On the resolution chart, the same pipeline retains 8.21% against the single step's 11.39% — a 28% relative regression. Same code, same kernels, opposite verdict, purely because the two images put their detail at different spatial frequencies.

The honest reading. Chained 2× upscaling is not a technique that helps or hurts; it is a coin flip whose bias depends on the image. If a vendor's documentation recommends it as a general rule, they are either describing a specific content type without saying so, or repeating something that was measured once on one image and never again.

The nearest-neighbour row is the interesting exception, and it is a trap. Chaining a 2× nearest pass before a Lanczos pass keeps 25–34% of the high-frequency energy, four to twenty times the Lanczos numbers. That energy is pixel-replication edges, not scene detail — which is exactly the same illusion described in Experiment 1, arrived at from the other direction. It also scores worse on PSNR than every other pipeline while showing the highest mean gradient. Three metrics, and the one that looks best is the one that is measuring the artifact.

When the effect is measured at larger factors it stays flat. At 8× (64 → 512), a three-step chain versus a single pass: PSNR 27.343 vs 27.342 on Mandelbrot, 9.309 vs 9.311 on the chart. The high-frequency retention moves a little — 12.10% → 12.68% on Mandelbrot, 4.62% → 5.99% on the chart — but both numbers are so far below the 65% that the ground truth carries that the difference is academic. At 2× there is nothing to chain at all, so the advice is untestable.

What the chaining costs in wall clock

Upscaling a 512×512 Mandelbrot to 4096×4096, best of three runs, ffmpeg 7.x on CPU: a single 8× Lanczos pass took 0.637 s; three chained 2× passes (512→1024→2048→4096) took 1.060 s. The chained version is 1.67× slower.

That is worth sitting with, because the chained pipeline actually does more arithmetic, not less. A single 8× pass touches 4096² output pixels in one separable filter. The chain touches 1024², then 2048², then 4096² — 1.31× the pixel work, since 0.375 of it is spent producing intermediates that get thrown away. The extra 0.67× of wall clock on top of that comes from reading and re-writing intermediates. So the advice costs compute, buys nothing on PSNR, and moves detail retention in a direction that depends on the image.

One caveat on scope, stated plainly: this measure covers resampling kernels of the same family. A genuinely different operator in the middle of the chain — a denoiser, a sharpener, a learned model — is not the same experiment and can legitimately change the answer. The claim being refuted here is the narrow one, that splitting a 4× rescale into two 2× rescales is inherently better. It is not. Pick a single pass, pick the cheapest one, and spend the saved time on getting a better source frame.

Experiment 4: of the detail an upscaler adds, how much was in the picture?

Everything above measured how much high-frequency energy a method produces. None of it asked whose energy it is. That is the hole this section fills, and it is the reason the earlier numbers look paradoxical: Lanczos scoring highest on PSNR while retaining 1.3% of the detail is only a contradiction if you assume the retained energy and the true detail are the same thing. They are not, and the gap between them is measurable.

The method is a band-by-band audit against a known answer. I built a 1024×1024 scene — a gradient, one hard-edged rectangle, and two stripe fields — and band-limited the whole thing to 0.45 cycles/pixel. The band limit is not decoration: it is the premise. A scene with no energy above 0.45 can be sampled on a 512×512 grid without aliasing, so "everything above 0.25 cycles/pixel" is exactly and only what a single 512 frame failed to capture. Nothing else is lost, and nothing in that band is an artefact of the sampling itself.

Then I modelled a capture: the scene through a Gaussian point-spread function (σ = 0.6 scene pixels), decimated 2× at four sub-pixel phases — (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1). One phase is a 512×512 frame. Four phases together tile the 1024 grid exactly, which the code verifies rather than assumes: the reassembled mosaic differs from the blurred scene by 0.0e+00.

For each candidate reconstruction I bandpass both it and the truth into four radial bands and report two numbers per band. corr is the normalised correlation with the truth inside that band: 1.0 means every cycle in that band was recovered, 0.0 means whatever energy sits there is unrelated to the scene. rms is the amplitude in the band, so you can see a method inflating a band while its correlation stays flat.

Three things in that table are worth stating in plain terms.

One: a single frame recovers 4.27% of what it lost. Below 0.25 cycles/pixel the upscale is honest — 0.996 correlation, essentially perfect. One band up, at 0.25–0.375, correlation collapses to 0.209. Energy-weighting the two upper bands gives a faithful share of 4.27%, which means 95.7% of everything above the source Nyquist is fabrication. The 33.61% of the scene's total energy that lives above 0.25 cycles/pixel is almost entirely gone, and what replaced it looks like detail without being any.

Two: sharpening is a volume knob on a channel with no signal. Sweeping the unsharp amount from 0 to 8 moves the upper-band correlation by 0.0001 — from 0.2085 to 0.2084 — while the amplitude in that band rises 8.99×. The amplitude gain is 1 + amount to within 0.2%, which is not a coincidence and not a property of images. A Gaussian of σ = 2 output pixels has an OTF of about 0.0008 at 0.3 cycles/pixel, so the unsharp mask is passing the band essentially unattenuated and adding it back at full weight. The measurement is reporting the kernel's own algebra. That is the point: there is no image information in the gain.

Three: the best amount of sharpening on this scene was zero. Sharpening does not add a single real cycle anywhere; it spends fidelity in the bands you had in order to inflate a band you never had. On real photographic output the low-band cost is smaller and the perceived improvement is real, because perception responds to edge contrast and not to correlation with an unavailable original. But the accounting is the accounting.

What it takes to get the upper band back

Four sub-pixel phases and no interpolation at all — just writing each sample into its true position on the 1024 grid — takes correlation at 0.25–0.375 from 0.209 to 0.987, and the faithful share of the lost band from 4.27% to 98.51%, with PSNR up 4.35 dB over the single frame. That is the same information the four captures held all along, placed correctly.

The interesting part is how few phases you need, and how badly phase choice matters. Two frames on the diagonal give 0.709; two frames on one axis give 0.362. Both are two frames. Both cost the same acquisition time. The diagonal pair doubles the sampling density in each direction; the horizontal pair only doubles it in one, and the upper band is a radial measure, so half of it stays empty. If you can only take two, take them diagonally.

Note that the two-frame rows have lower PSNR than the single frame while having far higher correlation in the upper bands. PSNR is dominated by the bands that carry most of the energy, and in a two-frame interleave half the output pixels are real samples while the other half are interpolated guesswork that lands on a coarser grid than a plain upscale would. The metric that matters for "did I recover detail" and the metric that matters for "is this pixel close to the truth" rank these four rows in different orders. Pick the metric before you pick the method.

The precision this needs, and the PSF you have to guess

A tenth of a pixel still leaves 0.809. A quarter of a pixel — which is a rounding error in most hand-held workflows — drops it to 0.363, below the two-frame diagonal case at far higher cost. The whole gain is available inside ±0.1 px and gone by ±0.5 px. This is why the technique lives in microscopy, astronomy and satellite imaging, where the platform is bolted down, and not in phone photography.

Deconvolution has a different failure mode: it needs the PSF, and the PSF is a guess. Feeding Richardson–Lucy the right σ (0.6) at 20 iterations reaches 40.73 dB. Guessing 0.4 or 0.8 — a 33% error in either direction — costs about 9 to 11 dB. Guessing 1.0 costs 16 dB. Notably, an underestimated σ is the safer direction and an overestimated one is not: at σ = 1.0 the upper band still correlates 0.954 and 0.883, which looks fine, while PSNR has collapsed to 24.95. A too-soft kernel asks the solver to invent smaller features than exist, and it obliges. The correlation in the top band is where that shows up first.

A check you can run without knowing the truth

All of the above needs a ground truth, which you do not have for a photograph. So: is there a test that separates recovered detail from invented detail using only the image itself? One candidate works and one does not, and the one that fails is the one people reach for.

What works: the round trip. Upscale, then downscale back to the source grid, and measure the residual against the frame you started from. Invented detail cannot survive that round trip coherently, because it does not correspond to anything the downsampler can find. Measured on the same single frame: residual 0.0018 with no sharpening, 0.0731 at amount 1.0, 0.2159 at 3.0, 0.5728 at 8.0. That is a 314× spread that tracks the amount of fabrication and requires no reference at all. If a tool's output does not survive being scaled back to its input size, the detail it added was never in the input.

What does not work: asking two upscalers to agree. The intuition is that independent methods converging on the same fine structure is evidence the structure is real. Measured: Lanczos and a B-spline cubic agree with each other at 1.000, 1.000, 0.982 and 0.929 across the four bands, while their correlation with the truth in those same bands is 0.996, 0.769, 0.209 and −0.046. In the top band they agree with each other 0.929 while being anti-correlated with the scene. They are not discovering the same hidden detail; they are computing the same deterministic function of the same insufficient samples, and the resulting ringing pattern is reproducible. Reproducibility is not truth.

The practical consequence. An interpolation upscaler cannot add a band it was never given — that is information theory, not opinion — and this audit puts a number on it: 4.27% of the lost band is real, and no amount of sharpening changes the numerator. What sharpening changes is how convincing the 95.7% looks. That is a legitimate goal for a thumbnail or a slide, and a serious problem for evidence, archiving, or anything where someone will later ask what was in the original pixels.

Reproducing the phase audit

Single file, numpy only, about two seconds, no dependencies beyond numpy. It prints the mosaic self-check first — if that line is not 0.0e+00, the premise is broken and every number below it is meaningless. The last two lines are the headline result, and they match the table above.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(20261115); N = 1024
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:N, 0:N].astype(float)
FY = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fftfreq(N)); RAD = np.hypot(FY[:, None], FY[None, :])

def band(img, lo, hi):                       # one radial band, mean removed
    F = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2(img - img.mean()))
    return np.real(np.fft.ifft2(np.fft.ifftshift(F * ((RAD >= lo) & (RAD < hi)))))

def limit(img, hi=0.45):                     # the premise: no energy above hi
    F = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2(img))
    return np.real(np.fft.ifft2(np.fft.ifftshift(F * (RAD <= hi))))

def psf(img, sigma=0.6):
    F = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2(img))
    return np.real(np.fft.ifft2(np.fft.ifftshift(
        F * np.exp(-2 * np.pi ** 2 * sigma ** 2 * RAD ** 2))))

def up2(src):                                # 2x lanczos3, PIL pixel centres
    s = N / src.shape[0]
    def ax(a, axis):
        c = (np.arange(N) + 0.5) / s - 0.5
        b = np.floor(c).astype(int)
        acc = np.zeros((N, a.shape[1]) if axis == 0 else (a.shape[0], N)); ws = 0
        for t in np.arange(-2, 4):
            w = np.sinc(c - b - t) * np.sinc((c - b - t) / 3.0)
            w[np.abs(c - b - t) >= 3] = 0
            shp = [1, 1]; shp[axis] = N
            acc += a.take(np.clip(b + t, 0, a.shape[axis] - 1), axis=axis) * w.reshape(shp)
            ws = ws + w
        return acc / (ws[:, None] if axis == 0 else ws[None, :])
    return ax(ax(np.asarray(src, float), 0), 1)

base  = 0.35 * (xx / N) + 0.25 * (yy / N)
base[(xx > 200) & (xx < 430) & (yy > 180) & (yy < 360)] += 0.45
base += 0.18 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * xx / 5.0) * (yy < 512)
base += 0.18 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (xx + yy) / 3.4) * (yy >= 512)
tex = limit(rng.normal(size=(N, N))); tex /= tex.std()
GT = limit(base + 0.10 * tex, 0.45); GT -= GT.mean()

BLUR = psf(GT, 0.6)
PH = [(0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1)]
frames = [BLUR[a::2, b::2].copy() for a, b in PH]

mosaic = np.zeros((N, N))                    # all four phases, zero interpolation
for (a, b), f in zip(PH, frames):
    mosaic[a::2, b::2] = f
print("mosaic == blurred truth: %.1e" % np.abs(mosaic - BLUR).max())   # must be 0.0e+00

def corr(c, lo, hi):
    a, b = band(c, lo, hi), band(GT, lo, hi)
    return (a * b).sum() / np.sqrt((a * a).sum() * (b * b).sum())

print("1 frame  b3 corr %.3f" % corr(up2(frames[0]), .25, .375))   # 0.209
print("4 phases b3 corr %.3f" % corr(mosaic, .25, .375))           # 0.987

Swap up2 for whichever resampler you are evaluating — the whole point is that the resampler is not what decides this, and the 4-phase row never calls it at all. The implementation above is a float64 separable Lanczos3 using PIL's pixel-centre geometry (output pixel i sits at (i+0.5)/scale - 0.5 in source coordinates, which is the half-pixel offset that cost me 46× on an earlier experiment). Results were unchanged by 8-bit quantisation: mapping the scene to 0–255, rounding, and mapping back moved the upper-band correlation from 0.209 to 0.208 and left PSNR at 21.77. The effect is 40 dB wide; quantisation is not the bottleneck.

Five bugs I hit, and the numbers they produced before I fixed them

This section exists because every one of these produced a publishable-looking table, and four of them pointed at the wrong conclusion.

  1. Fourier-domain convolution with a shifted kernel. My radial frequency grid is built from fftshift(fftfreq), which is in shifted order, but np.fft.fft2 returns unshifted output. Multiplying the two directly is a silent no-op on the kernel: the convolution of a constant field with the PSF returned 0.0286 instead of 1.0, so the Richardson–Lucy update gained a factor of 35 every iteration. Reported PSNR: −2033 dB, then nan. An assert that conv(ones) == 1 now runs before the first iteration, and it is what caught the bug that produced −66 dB in an earlier version of the same function.
  2. Signed data into an algorithm that assumes photons. Richardson–Lucy is derived for non-negative intensities. My scene has negative values, so the ratio data / (PSF * estimate) flipped sign wherever the scene went below zero and the iteration diverged. Shifting by the minimum before solving and back afterwards fixes it, at zero cost, because a constant passes through a unit-sum convolution unchanged.
  3. A scene that was not actually band-limited. I band-limited the texture but not the hard-edged rectangle, which put real energy above 0.5 cycles/pixel into a scene I was about to decimate 2×. The result aliased, so "everything above 0.25 was lost" was false before I measured anything. Caught by checking that the mosaic matched the blurred truth exactly — it differed by 0.58. After band-limiting the whole scene: 0.0e+00.
  4. A parity flip disguised as a registration error. To add ε of misalignment I shifted the signal by (nominal phase + ε) and then sampled at the nominal parity. Shifting by a whole pixel and then reading the other parity samples a different row — so a 0.01 px error produced a residual of 0.58 and reported the technique as collapsing under a hundredth of a pixel. The correct model shifts by ε only and samples at the nominal parity. After the fix, 0.01 px is invisible and the real tolerance is the 0.1 px shown above — 100× more forgiving than the broken version claimed.
  5. PIL's unsharp mask is 8-bit only. ImageFilter.UnsharpMask raises ValueError: image has wrong mode on a float image, and rounding to 8 bits first would have destroyed exactly the sub-quantisation differences the experiment measures. Replaced with an explicit Gaussian in float via the FFT.

Two of these are worth generalising. The shifted-kernel bug and the parity bug are both cases where the code ran, produced plausible numbers, and was wrong by a factor of a hundred or a sign. Neither would have been caught by looking at the output; both were caught by asserting a property that must hold — convolution of a constant returns the constant, a mosaic of four phases reproduces the signal — and checking it before trusting anything downstream. If a measurement has a premise, test the premise.

What this means when you pick a tool

Given the above, the useful questions are not "how many times upscale" or "which algorithm." They are:

  1. Is this interpolation or reconstruction? If the tool has no model weights and no training data, it is interpolation and it cannot add detail. That is fine for enlarging a screenshot for a slide. It is useless for "restoring" a photo.
  2. What is my actual scale factor? From the table above, 2× retains roughly 17× more high-frequency energy than 4×. If your source is 360p and your target is 4K, you are asking for 12×, and no method on this page is going to give you a good answer. The honest move is to change the target, not the kernel.
  3. Am I judging at 100% zoom? A 4× bicubic upscale looks acceptable scaled to fit a phone screen, because the screen itself is the low-pass filter. Judge at 1:1 or you are evaluating your display, not the file.

The uncomfortable conclusion. Reconstruction models are the only tools that can add plausible detail, and by definition the detail they add was not in your source. They are inference, and inference can be confidently wrong — a face that gains convincing eyelashes it never had, a texture that becomes wood grain when it was fabric. That is a real trade and it should be a deliberate one, not a surprise.

Reproducing this

Both earlier experiments are ffmpeg + numpy, no proprietary tooling. The shape of the commands:

# build the test pattern, shrink 4x, upscale back with each kernel
ffmpeg -i source.png -vf "scale=100:100:flags=lanczos" low.png
ffmpeg -i low.png   -vf "scale=400:400:flags=bicubic" up_bicubic.png

# PSNR against ground truth
ffmpeg -i up_bicubic.png -i source.png \
  -lavfi "[0:v]scale=400:400:flags=bicubic[x];[x][1:v]psnr" -f null -

# high-frequency energy: 2-D FFT, ratio of energy outside r>0.18*N to inside

The high-frequency metric is the sum of FFT magnitude outside a radius of 0.18 × image width, divided by the sum inside it. It is not a standard metric — I built it because PSNR and SSIM both failed to distinguish "smooth" from "detailed" in a way that matched what the eye sees. Treat it as a relative comparison between methods on the same image, not as an absolute quality score.
